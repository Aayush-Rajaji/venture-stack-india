Product — What Exists, What Is Next, How It Is Built

What a customer can actually use today versus what is still a slide, the roadmap, technical approach and dependencies, IP position, and the safety constraints that apply.

Product

The discipline this file enforces: a hard line between what exists and what is planned. Founders blur them without meaning to — the demo becomes "our platform," the roadmap becomes "our capabilities" — and then get caught out by one specific question in a diligence call.

The vocabulary, used correctly

Getting these wrong in a pitch is a small thing that reads as a large one:

TermWhat it actually means
PrototypeDemonstrates the idea. Not for real users. Often held together by hand.
Proof of concept (PoC)Proves the hard technical thing is possible. Answers "can this work?"
MVPThe smallest thing a real customer can get real value from. Answers "will they use it?"
PilotA structured test of a PoC or MVP, with real users, over a defined period, against defined success criteria. A pilot is not a product — it is the experiment you run on one.
Commercial productSold, supported, and expected to work without you in the room.

"Do you own it?" is not rhetorical. If an agency built your app, an intern designed your logo, or a friend wrote the code, the intellectual property may not be yours by default. Assignment has to be written down. This surfaces during diligence, at the worst possible moment, and it is cheap to fix now.

A verbal maintenance promise is not a maintenance agreement. If a vendor has committed to supporting what they built, get it in writing with a response time, an uptime expectation, source access, and a handover clause. "He said he'd fix anything for two years" is a conversation, not a contract.

Record the reasoning, not just the decision. "We are not filing because the design changes every quarter and the money is better spent on customers, and we accept the copying risk" is a defensible position. Having no position is not.

Trade secrets — what are you deliberately not disclosing, and how is that actually protected? A formula everyone in the workshop knows and nobody has signed an agreement about is not a trade secret.

Human in the loop

If any part of your product makes or recommends a decision automatically — especially using AI — answer this explicitly:

What the system decides on its own[TBD]
What a qualified human must validate first[TBD]
Who that human is[TBD]
What the user is told about the limits[TBD]

Treat any automated system as capable of being confidently wrong, regardless of how well it has been trained or guarded. If an output could harm someone — a health suggestion, a financial recommendation, a safety instruction — there must be a named point where a qualified person validates it before it reaches the user. Buyers, regulators and serious investors all ask this question, and "the model is very accurate" is not an answer to it.

Sustainability and impact claims

If you make environmental, ethical or social claims, they must be precise, current, and survivable under scrutiny.

Claim you makeActually true today?EvidenceWhat is aspirational
[TBD]

Greenwashing is when a term is used to make something sound sustainable that mostly is not — "vegan leather" that is predominantly plastic being the canonical example. The route through is not to overclaim or to go quiet, but to be transparent about the stage the product is actually at. A proprietary formulation or process does not have to be disclosed. What the product is, what is still conventional or synthetic, and what the actual goal is, do. Customers forgive a company that is honestly part of the way there. They do not forgive one that claimed to have arrived and got checked.
